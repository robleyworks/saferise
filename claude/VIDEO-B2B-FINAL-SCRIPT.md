## 1 · Structure

The employee speaks in the first person. The narrator answers immediately, and
answers to the buyer — naming what that state cost the organisation, in money,
people or reputation. The pattern runs four times, then the film turns to the
method.

| | |
|---|---|
| 00 | The narrator sets the frame and names the cost up front |
| 01 / 01b | Bluntness → two resignations, sick leave, a public review |
| 02 / 02b | Promotion → a client-facing error, a performance conversation |
| 03 / 03b | Overwhelm → thinner work, a notice sitting in drafts |
| 04 / 04b | Not himself in the room → the handover, the feedback, the decision |
| 05–08 | The argument, then the protocol |
| 09–12 | Access, the 8 + 2 model, the sector wall |
| 13 | The close |

## 2 · The script

### 00 · NARRATOR — 16.5s

> You hired them for their expertise. And you've seen what it looks like when
> they have it. But there are days when none of it is available to them — and
> the company registers the consequences of that.

### 01 · EMPLOYEE — 9.1s

> You know, I just speak direct. I don't really care how people feel about it.
> This is just how I talk.

### 01b · NARRATOR — 28.7s

> Two people on the same team stopped speaking properly in March over a
> misunderstanding. Nothing was escalated — no major incident, no policy broken.
> But by July one had resigned and the other was on a second period of sick
> leave. In August a customer got the end of that chain and left the review that
> is still the first thing anyone reads about your company.

### 02 · EMPLOYEE — 14.3s

> Well, you know, after I got promoted — it was a good thing, but it broke me.
> All my best skills were in my old position. Now I'm a fish out of water.

### 02b · NARRATOR — 16.1s

> You promoted your best supervisor. At four months they signed off something
> they would have caught in their old job, and it reached the client. They are
> now the person you are having a performance conversation about.

### 03 · EMPLOYEE — 9.6s

> It's just overwhelming here every day. The amount of pressure I deal with. And
> then I'm to blame for how I react.

### 03b · NARRATOR — 12.2s

> The work still goes out, so nobody has asked. It goes out later, and thinner.
> Her notice is written. It has been in a drafts folder since June.

### 04 · EMPLOYEE — 6.1s

> I am not myself in the room that matters. My best thinking arrives afterwards.

### 04b · NARRATOR — 16.5s

> The handover after a bad shift, given while still shaking. The feedback that
> turned into defensiveness before the second sentence was out. The decision
> taken while exhausted, by somebody who would have decided differently on a
> calmer day.

### 05 · NARRATOR — 3.9s

> The training is not missing. Access to it is.

### 06 · NARRATOR — 10.9s

> Frameworks and leadership models assume attention is available. SafeRise works
> one layer earlier — the state a person is in while the moment is happening.

### 07 · NARRATOR — 17.4s

> An assistance programme waits for someone to make the request, then routes
> them to a stranger. The solution arrives as exposure — to a meeting. But an
> adult nervous system does not reorganise from exposure. They reorganise under
> attention, repeated.

### 08 · NARRATOR — 23.0s

> That is what a SafeRise protocol offers. Our self-guided protocols are
> designed for the specific states — during, after, or before it takes over.
> Working it down with insights, breath and attention, so they can come back
> with clarity, and the capacity to judge what is actually there and make the
> best choice.

### 09 · NARRATOR — 14.3s

> With access to our full library, every employee is covered from the first day.
> No referral, no waiting list, no waiting for opening hours, and no limit on
> how many need it at once.

### 10 · NARRATOR — 19.6s

> Everyone gets the foundation. Then one built for what they do, and one built
> for where they do it. A nurse on a ward, a teacher at three in the afternoon
> and a front-desk supervisor in a resort do not have the same bad hour.

### 11 — five seconds, no narration — the sector wall builds

### 12 · NARRATOR — 5.7s

> Talk to us about getting SafeRise protocols for your roles and your sectors.

### 13 · NARRATOR — 18.3s

> You fund access. They own what happens inside it. What comes back to you is
> the hour, met by somebody who can reach their training, their judgement, and
> represent your organisation on and off the clock. Request a free consultation,
> today.

## 3 · Notes

**00 names the cost in the first fifteen seconds** — *the company registers the
consequences of that*. It tells the buyer this is a P&L conversation, not a
wellbeing one.

**The consequences are specific and none of them are claims about SafeRise.**
A resignation, a second period of sick leave, a public review, a client-facing
error, a notice in a drafts folder. They describe what an unregulated state
costs. The film never says SafeRise prevents any of them.

**04b is the old v10 strain trio, kept.** The three lines that were beats 03–05
in v10 now sit together as the narrator's answer to beat 04.

**07 keeps the exposure argument** that v10 flagged as the first candidate for
cutting. It survived because 08 was rewritten to land the method concretely
straight after it.

**Typos cleaned, nothing else:** *cusotmer, protcols, saefrise, reprsent, an
adult nervous systems*, and *come back with room with clarity* → *come back with
clarity*.

## 4 · The two final corrections

Beat 09 read *"every employee gets access from the first day"* — **access**
twice in one sentence, since the beat opens *with access to our full library*.
Corrected to **is covered from the first day**.

Beat 13 closed on *"Call for a free consultation today."* A call implies a
person answering, which is a delivery commitment, and `POSITIONING.md` §6 flags
roughly four hours a month of facilitator capacity as the binding constraint.
**Request a free consultation, today** takes a form as easily as a call.
