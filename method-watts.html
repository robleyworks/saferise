01What the idea says

The struggle is the second problem

Watts's recurring point is that a great deal of suffering is not the difficulty itself but the effort spent objecting to it. There is the feeling, and then there is the fight with the feeling — and the second is usually the larger expense and the one entirely within your gift.

His image for it is trying to stand still in moving water, or the backwards law: the harder you pursue certain states, the more reliably you drive them off. Insisting on sleep produces wakefulness. Demanding calm produces agitation about not being calm. Some things only arrive when you stop reaching for them, and effort is the wrong instrument.

He is careful about what this is not. Non-resistance is not passivity, not approval, and not indifference. You can act, decisively, and still not be at war with the fact that the situation exists. The distinction is between doing something about a thing and refusing to admit it is happening — and only the second is what he means by resistance.

None of this is a scientific claim and Watts made no pretence that it was. He was a popular interpreter of Daoist and Buddhist material, and a contested one. What SafeRise takes is one observation about effort, which a person can check against their own experience in about a minute.

The first problemThe state itself Whatever has arrived. Often not chosen, often not immediately changeable, and frequently smaller than what gets built on top of it.
The second problemThe fight with it Objecting, arguing, insisting it should not be here. This is where most of the energy goes, and it is the half you are actually paying for.
What is leftThe thing, without the fight Still unwelcome. Considerably lighter, and now something you can act on rather than something you are braced against.

Dropping the second does not require liking the first. That is the whole move, and it is why Step 03 says accept rather than approve.

02What SafeRise takes

Accept is not approve

Step 03 carries the word accept, and this framework is why it survives every review. The alternative words are worse: allow sounds permissive, surrender sounds like defeat, let go sounds like a thing you can do on request. Accept is the one that means stop spending force on the fact of it, which is the actual instruction.

Step 01

Recognise

Not this framework, though it sets up the conditions. Naming without arguing is already a small non-resistance, but the step itself comes from polyvagal theory and Compassionate Inquiry.

Step 02

Regulate

Not this framework — with one exception worth naming. The instruction to let the breath be easy rather than forced is this idea applied to breathing. Chasing the rate defeats it.

Step 03

Release

Load-bearing, shared with Compassionate Inquiry. Maté supplies the register the question is asked in; this supplies what you do with the answer — stop paying for the fight, and act on what is left.

Step 04

Rise

Not this framework, and slightly in tension with it. Rehearsing forward is a form of reaching, and this idea is sceptical of reaching. The tension is real and the method holds both rather than resolving it.

04What register this is

One observation, and an interpreter

Where it sits

Three things this gives the method — and what it is not.

Interpretive band, like Jung. There is no experimental literature here and none is implied. What there is instead is an observation you can test on yourself immediately — which is a different kind of warrant, and worth less than evidence, and still worth something.

  • Resistance is a separable, and payable, cost The state and the fight with the state are two things. Separating them is the entire content of Step 03, and the part a person can actually act on.
  • Some states are defeated by reaching for them Calm, sleep, ease. This is why nothing here is timed against a target and why no protocol promises you will feel better by the end.
  • Acceptance and action are compatible You can stop objecting to a fact and still change it. Holding that line is what keeps the third step from reading as resignation.
  • What it is not: a claim about anything No mechanism, no evidence base, no outcome. If the observation does not match your experience, nothing on this platform requires you to accept it.

Watts described himself as an entertainer and a populariser rather than a scholar or a practitioner, and that is the right way to read him. His gift was phrasing — putting something very old into English a Western reader could actually use.

The idea is not his, and the page says so because the credit belongs elsewhere. Non-resistance runs through Daoist and Zen material by many centuries. The attribution here is to the interpreter, not to the origin, and the older sources are in the reading list because they are the deeper ones.

05Read it yourself

Read the arguments yourself

  • Watts, A. (1951). The Wisdom of Insecurity. Pantheon. The clearest statement of the argument, and the shortest. Start here. Primary source
  • Watts, A. (1957). The Way of Zen. Pantheon. The fuller treatment, and where he sets out the tradition at length. Primary source
  • Watts, A. (1975). Tao: The Watercourse Way. Pantheon. Completed after his death. Where the water image is developed most fully. Primary source
  • Laozi (c. 4th century BCE). Tao Te Ching. The origin of wu wei — effortless action. The idea Watts is interpreting, roughly two and a half millennia earlier. Origin
  • Suzuki, S. (1970). Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind. Weatherhill. A practitioner rather than an interpreter, on the same territory. The deeper source, if the idea holds your interest. Origin
  • Hayes, S. C., Strosahl, K. & Wilson, K. G. (2011). Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, 2nd ed. Guilford Press. Where the same distinction — accepting a state while still acting — was given an experimental treatment. The evidence for this idea, if you want it, is here. Evidence base
